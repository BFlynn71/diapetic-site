Ketones and DKA in diabetic dogs and cats: what to record and when to call your vet

By the developer of DiaPetic, who cares for Obi, a diabetic Sheepadoodle · Updated November 23, 2026

When a diabetic pet's body cannot use sugar for energy, often because there is not enough insulin working, it starts breaking down fat instead. That makes ketones. A build-up of ketones can lead to diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a serious emergency that needs a veterinarian.

Some vets ask owners to test for ketones at home, especially in the first weeks after a diagnosis or when a pet is unwell. This guide covers how home tests work and what to record. Your vet decides when to test, what the results mean and what to do next.

Signs that mean call your vet now

Call your veterinarian or an emergency clinic right away if your diabetic pet:

  • Will not eat, or eats much less than usual
  • Is vomiting
  • Seems weak, very tired or unlike themselves
  • Is drinking or urinating much more than usual
  • Has breath that smells sweet or like nail polish remover
  • Is breathing fast or deeply

Do not wait for a ketone test if your pet shows these signs. A negative test does not rule out a problem.

When vets ask for a ketone test

Ask your vet whether they want you to test for ketones at home, and when. Many ask for a test when a pet is not eating, is vomiting or seems unwell, or when glucose readings are higher than usual. If your cat takes an oral diabetes medicine instead of insulin, your vet may ask you to check ketones regularly, because ketones can build up even when glucose readings look normal. Write down their answer so everyone who helps with care can find it.

Urine strips and blood meters

  • Urine strips change color when ketones are in the urine. Dip the strip in fresh urine, wait the time printed on the bottle, then compare it with the color chart. Results are words such as Negative, Trace, Small, Moderate or Large.
  • Blood ketone meters measure ketones in a drop of blood and show a number in mmol/L. Ask your vet which meter to use and which number they want to hear about.

Urine strips do not detect the main ketone the body makes, so they can read lower than a blood meter, especially early on. Veterinary guidelines note that blood meters pick up the early stages of DKA more accurately. Tell your vet which test you used.

What to write down

  • The time of the test, which test you used and the result
  • A glucose reading taken at the same time, if you check glucose at home
  • Whether your pet ate, and how much
  • When the last insulin shot was given
  • Vomiting, weakness, drinking or anything else unusual
  • What your vet said to do

Do not change or skip an insulin dose because of a ketone result. Call your veterinarian and follow their instructions.
